Water Heater Anode Rod Replacement: A La Puente, CA Homeowner Guide

The anode rod is the cheap part that sacrifices itself so your tank does not rust. Checking it on schedule can add years to a water heater.

Written by La Puente Pro Plumbing · Updated November 2026

Inside most tank water heaters is a metal rod, usually magnesium or aluminum, threaded into the top of the tank. It corrodes on purpose. Because it is more reactive than the steel tank, it attracts the corrosion that would otherwise eat the tank wall. Once the rod is gone, the tank itself starts to rust.

Replacing a spent rod is one of the cheapest ways to extend the life of a tank, but only if the tank is still sound. This guide covers how the rod works, how often to check it, what replacement involves and when it is too late.

Function

How an anode rod protects the tank

Water in a heater is hot and full of dissolved minerals and oxygen, which makes steel rust. The anode rod gives up metal ions instead, corroding in place of the tank lining. The rod is a consumable part, designed to be used up.

Different rod materials suit different water. Magnesium rods protect well but can react with some water chemistry. Aluminum rods last longer in some conditions. Your plumber can suggest a type for your water and your tank.

Schedule

How often to check the rod

Many manufacturers suggest checking the rod every few years, and sooner in hard water. The rod is spent when it is mostly bare steel core, deeply pitted or thin. A plumber can pull the rod to look at it, which takes a short visit.

If you have never checked the rod and your heater is several years old, it is worth a look. The check costs far less than a new tank.

Process

How replacement works

The plumber turns off power or gas, closes the cold water inlet and drains a little water to lower the level below the rod. The rod sits in a hex head on top of the tank, and removing it often takes a long breaker bar or an impact tool. Clearance above the tank matters, because some rods are long and need room to come out.

Where there is no room, a flexible segmented rod can be used. The new rod goes in with thread sealant rated for the job, the tank is refilled and checked for leaks, and power or gas is restored. See water heater repair for related service work.

Too late

When replacement is no longer worth it

If the tank has been leaking, has rust in the water or is old and badly scaled, a new rod will not save it. The same goes for a tank that has run for years with a spent rod, since the damage is already done. In that case, water heater replacement is the honest answer, and the plumber should say so.

Smell

Rotten egg smell and anode rods

A sulfur smell in hot water can come from bacteria reacting with a magnesium rod. A plumber may suggest a different rod type or a tank flush. The smell is usually only in the hot water, which separates it from a problem in the supply.

Quick guide

Anode rod condition and what to do

A simple way to read what the plumber finds.

Rod conditionMeaningAction
Thick, mostly intactStill protecting the tankCheck again later
Thin with bare spotsNearing the endReplace the rod
Mostly bare steel coreSpentReplace now if tank is sound
Tank leaking or rustedDamage is doneReplace the heater

Anode rods and the water in La Puente

The La Puente Valley County Water District reported a pH of 7.9 in its 2023 water quality report, and says its supply draws mostly on wells in the Main San Gabriel Basin. Water chemistry like this affects how fast a rod is used up, and a plumber can adjust the schedule once they see your rod.

The tank and its fittings follow the California Plumbing Code, based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC). Your address may be served by a different provider, so check your bill and ask for that provider's report.

California also requires water heaters to be braced, anchored or strapped against earthquake motion under Health and Safety Code section 19211, which is worth checking whenever the heater is serviced.
